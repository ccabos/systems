# The Five Diagnostic Questions

Once a change is proposed — a manifesto pledge, a board strategy, a
reform programme — five questions establish whether it can reach the
operating loop. They mirror the five in
`../control-structures/diagnostic-questions.md`, which interrogate the
loop this one acts on. Answering each well requires evidence, not
assertion.

## 1. Which rule changes?

> *Which written rule, deadline, competence, budget line, post or
> measure is different afterwards?*

A proposal that names none of these is a statement of direction, not
a change. Direction is not a cause: a goal produces an effect only
once it has been translated into a change to the system's connections
(see `knowledge/foundations/system-definition.md`).

The test is substitution. If the proposal can be restated without
naming any rule and lose nothing, no rule was named.

A good answer identifies the rule precisely enough that someone could
draft the amendment.

## 2. Who owns the actuator — and is it the controller?

> *Who signs the change into force, and is that the same actor who is
> proposing it?*

Ownership means signature, not responsibility. The head of a
government does not own state building law; a chief executive does not
own the objectives scheme alone. Where the answer is "someone else",
the proposal is not a control action but a *request* to another
controller, and the loop has an extra segment nobody has analysed.

A good answer names the signing actor for each lever, and states what
the proposing controller can actually do to that actor — appoint,
fund, legislate, negotiate, or nothing.

## 3. What is the change latency, and what is the effect latency?

> *How long from decision to the rule being in force, and how long
> from there to an observable change in outcomes?*

The two are separate and often inverse. A statutory deadline can be
amended in months and bites immediately. Funded training places can be
decided in weeks and change nothing for years. Reporting a single
figure hides which of the two is the binding constraint, and the two
take different remedies.

Both are orders of magnitude. State them as brackets and read
`../linear-algebra/false-precision-trap.md` before turning them into
a schedule.

A good answer gives both, separately, for every lever, and names which
one dominates.

## 4. Who loses something?

> *Which actor gives up a competence, a budget, or a certainty?*

Every rule change redistributes. A competence moved to another
authority is a competence taken from one. A disclosure duty removes a
certainty. An unopposed reform proposal is usually one whose losers
have not been identified yet, not one that has no losers.

A good answer names the losing actors and states whether they hold any
of the actuators from question 2 — because when they do, questions 2
and 4 are the same question and the proposal is in trouble.

## 5. Which channel would show the effect, and within what time?

> *What feedback would establish that the change worked, how long
> until it carries signal, and is that shorter than the controller's
> tenure?*

If nobody measures processing time, nobody will discover that
processing time did not fall. Worse, if the channel carries signal
only after the controller has gone, the loop is structurally open: the
control action is issued by someone who will never receive its
feedback. See Pattern 2 in `dangerous-patterns.md`.

This question is where the change loop connects back to the operating
loop, because the channel in question is one of the feedback channels
interrogated by question 1 of
`../control-structures/diagnostic-questions.md`.

A good answer names the channel, its operator, its latency, and the
controller's remaining tenure — and says plainly when the second
exceeds the third.

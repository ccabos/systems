# STAMP — References

Primary sources for STAMP and STPA as developed by Nancy Leveson
and collaborators at MIT.

## Books

- Leveson, N.G. (2012). *Engineering a Safer World: Systems
  Thinking Applied to Safety*. MIT Press.
  The foundational text for STAMP. Available open-access from the
  MIT Partnership for Systems Approaches to Safety and Security
  (PSAS).

- Leveson, N.G. & Thomas, J.P. (2018). *STPA Handbook*. MIT
  Partnership for Systems Approaches to Safety and Security.
  The procedural reference for applying STPA step by step. Also
  open-access from PSAS.

## Papers

- Leveson, N.G. (2004). "A New Accident Model for Engineering
  Safer Systems." *Safety Science*, 42(4), 237–270.
  The original journal article introducing the STAMP model.

## Antecedents and adjacent literature

- Rasmussen, J. (1997). "Risk Management in a Dynamic Society: A
  Modelling Problem." *Safety Science*, 27(2–3), 183–213.
  Leveson's acknowledged foundation. The sociotechnical control
  hierarchy (government → regulators → company → management →
  staff → work) and the concept of systems *drifting* toward the
  boundary of acceptable performance both originate here.
- Perrow, C. (1984). *Normal Accidents*. Basic Books. The
  structural (interactive complexity × tight coupling) account of
  accidents; the sociological counterpart to STAMP's control
  account.
- Vaughan, D. (1996). *The Challenger Launch Decision*. University
  of Chicago Press. Field evidence for process-model drift
  ("normalization of deviance") inside a real control structure.
- Dekker, S. (2011). *Drift into Failure*. Ashgate. The
  complexity-theoretic restatement; useful on why component-level
  fixes fail.

## Relation to this project

The project uses STAMP/STPA as the hazard analysis technique for
non-technical systems. The adaptation is conservative: the
vocabulary and procedure are used unchanged; the novelty is only
that the controllers and controlled processes are social rather
than mechanical. It is worth stating how small that step is:
Leveson's own control hierarchies already contain legislatures,
regulators, courts, unions, and management as controllers
[Leveson2012, ch. 4], and Rasmussen's 1997 hierarchy is explicitly
sociotechnical from top to bottom. What is genuinely non-standard
here is only the *subject matter* (systems with no engineered core
at all — religions, families, polities) and the justificatory-rung
extension (`../justification-rungs/`), which is project-internal.
